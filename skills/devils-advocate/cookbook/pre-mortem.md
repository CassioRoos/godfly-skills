# Pre-mortem Framing: Making Failure Vivid

**The full pre-mortem process lives in the `premortem-postmortem` skill - use that to run one.** This cookbook keeps the one thing that makes any pre-mortem work: framing the failure so vividly that people stop defending the plan and start analyzing the wreckage.

## Why Vivid Framing Works

Asking "what could go wrong?" triggers defensiveness - people protect the plan. Saying "it failed - why?" reframes criticism as analysis. Failure is no longer a possibility to argue about; it's a fact to explain. Research shows this prospective-hindsight framing increases the ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by about 30%.

## Writing the Failure Statement

Transport yourself to the future where the project has already failed.

**Setup statement**:
> "It's [date 6-12 months out]. The project has failed completely. Not partially - completely. It's being shut down as a total failure. We're here to understand why."

**Key mindset shifts**:
- Failure is a fact, not a possibility
- We're analyzing, not preventing (yet)
- All concerns are valid data

### Make it vivid

Don't say "the project might fail." Say:

> "The project has failed. The team is demoralized. The company lost $2M. Your boss is asking what happened."

The concrete details - the date, the number, the boss's question - are what unlock honest answers.

### Write complete statements

Failure reasons must be specific enough to act on:
- Not: "timeline"
- But: "We underestimated integration complexity and missed the launch window"

### Include the embarrassing ones

"We failed because we ignored obvious warning signs everyone saw but nobody mentioned." The concerns people are hesitant to voice are often the most valuable - the framing exists precisely to give them permission.

## Sorting What You Surface

Don't score failure reasons on likelihood/impact matrices. Sort them the way `premortem-postmortem` does:

- **Real threats** - plausible failure modes that deserve prevention or monitoring
- **Noise** - imagined failures that don't survive a second look
- **Avoided truths** - the ones everyone already suspected but nobody had said out loud; these are the pre-mortem's real payload

Then hand the real threats and avoided truths to the `premortem-postmortem` process for prevention planning.

## Common Framing Mistakes

- **Not committing to failure**: Hedging with "might" instead of "did" - the whole technique collapses
- **Focusing only on external factors**: "The market changed" vs. "We didn't adapt"
- **Stopping at surface reasons**: "Timeline slipped" vs. "We underestimated X because Y"
- **Making it comfortable**: If nothing in the failure statement stings, it isn't vivid enough
